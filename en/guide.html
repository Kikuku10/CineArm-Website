One user's field notes

Buying guide

I am a hobbyist — not a developer, not a company. When I bought my first film-arm kit I assumed the hardware would be the problem; what actually stopped me was the software. What came in the box was a large teach pendant.

What I actually needed cost five figures, and was sold separately per operating system — so I wrote my own. Once it worked I found I was not the only one stuck at that step, so I lowered the gate: no payment first, download and try it. Nothing here is being sold; these are simply the pitfalls, written down.

In 30 seconds

  • A working system is four parts: arm, camera plate, base, software. The last three decide the image — and the word “kit” tends to hide them.
  • A used 5 kg-class arm runs 10k–30k CNY; a used 10 kg-class 20k–30k; new is roughly double; turnkey kits have no list price.
  • The base is the part that eats you alive — mine cost a month of factory back-and-forth, harder than writing the software.
  • Feeling it at rest proves nothing: extend fully and run at medium-high speed — that is when vibration shows.
  • Identify the vibration source: arm, base, or camera stabilisation. They look identical and they are not the robot's fault alike.
  • Wire it if you can: wireless latency makes the arm itself judder, not just the picture.
  • The camera plate can be free: my CAD is open source, and JLCPCB CNC sampling comes to about 30 CNY.
  • When something fails: export the log, shoot a video, and describe reproducible steps — it beats “it does not work” ten times over.

1. What actually happened

Start with my mistakes — every lesson below came out of them.

01

Bought a film-arm kit

A Nova 5 with bundled software and accessories. It looked complete. Later I learned that new kits run the same playbook — you buy the arm, the rest is your problem.

02

The software disappointed

It could set nodes, speed and smoothing — the same things the official pendant software already does. I wanted an editable speed curve, a lock onto the subject and follow-focus linkage. It could not. It was a large teach pendant.

03

Account rebinding was worse

The login was still tied to the previous owner's number and could not be changed; switching computers hit a binding limit. Specific to turnkey kits — an arm bought alone has no account to bind.

04

Accessories were questionable

The bundled camera plate locked on a single thin screw. I never trusted it — eventually I drew my own and open-sourced it.

05

Sold the Nova 5, bought a CR10A

After a while I sold the Nova 5 and paid 25k CNY for a lab-retired CR10A — 10 kg payload, 1300 mm reach, far more comfortable with a mid-size body.

06

A month lost to the base

The arm arrived with no base, so it could not even start. Two factories, wrong dimensions, welded sub-plates, corners cut — a full month gone. Harder than writing the software from scratch.

A kit saves procurement time, but “can it shoot what I want” is a different question.

The base: a month of my life

The most underestimated part of the whole system. My goal was one base that fits CR5 / Nova 5 / CR10 / CR20 — get the hole pattern right once and future arms just bolt on.

First orderSent the drawing; it came back and would not fit. Hole pattern off. Returned.
Second factorySame story: welded sub-plates, a base that was not flat, corners cut, one revision dragging into the next.
The costThe CR10A arrived and could not run — a month with no usable base.
In the endJust over 1,000 CNY for a 100-plus kg solid steel base that finally holds still.
Lesson

Put tolerances, hole pattern and flatness on the drawing; find a shop that reads drawings and will rework; budget a month. Honestly, that month of coordination cost more than writing the software.

I never trusted the single-screw plate, so I drew a new one — it takes common Arca and SmallRig quick releases, and the CAD is on MakerWorld.

4. Used inspection: six things to check

Feeling it at rest tells you nothing — make it move.

1. VibrationUsually appears only in motion. Extend fully and run at medium-high speed — the combination that exposes it.
2. Find the sourceArm compliance (slight is normal), base stiffness and camera stabilisation all look alike. Do not blame the robot first.
3. Brake typePlunger brakes have more play; electromagnetic brakes lock when power drops and hold stiffer. For film work, prefer electromagnetic.
4. Joint travel±180° or ±360° on J4/J5/J6 decides whether the camera can roll over. Firmware revisions also behave differently near limits.
5. Collision sensitivityIt has to be adjustable down, or the arm alarms on the slightest contact and is unusable on set.
6. Payload parametersMust match reality. Get them wrong and it sags or feels floaty — that is not a broken arm, it is a wrong number.

Bring CineArm to the inspection

Rehearse on the virtual arm first — record positions, drag the timeline, set a target, connect follow focus. None of it needs hardware. On site, connect the real arm and run the same two positions.

Cover six checks at onceLow-speed vibration, noise, limits and brake hold in one pass.
Learn the fitProtocol revision, feedback correctness, limit readouts — visible as soon as it connects.
Know it before you buyNo learning curve on installation day.
No account wallAn original Nova arm has no account restriction. Turnkey kit software may be locked to an account, leaving you unable to connect at all.
Two conditions

The seller agrees to power it up, and the area is clear with the E-stop in hand. Do not drop someone else's machine while testing.

Learn the official pendant software first

A number of arm parameters live only in the manufacturer's pendant software — drag damping, speed ceiling, collision sensitivity, limit configuration. They sit outside the motion software's scope, but they decide everything you see during inspection.

How

Download the pendant software and request a virtual licence (demo/simulation), then walk through the interface in the simulator. On site you will know which values to check and which are locked — the inspection goes far faster, and you are harder to fob off.

5. Software: the most underestimated part

It decides whether you are driving a robot or directing a shot.

Most kit software stops at pendant levelIt records points and plays them back, but cannot edit a speed curve, hold an aim, or drive follow focus.
A simple testCan it schedule a shot on a timeline, like an editor? If yes it is motion software; if not it is a controller.
Turnkey kits add account bindingChanging computers or phone numbers may be restricted; sourcing arm and software separately avoids this layer.
Ask about protocol and firmwareProtocol revision, extension APIs, updatability — third-party software often stops connecting after an upgrade.

If you plan to build it yourself, this is where most of the work is — and it is the part that shapes the final image.

6. Safety: for personal use, prefer a collaborative arm

Personal experience, not an industry verdict.

Built for one personPersonal shoots rarely have a safety supervisor; collision detection and compliant stopping are far safer when something goes wrong.
Start slow and emptyConfirm compatibility before power-up; the first run should be low speed, no load, clear area.
E-stop in reachA physical E-stop within arm's length, and a brake that provably holds on power loss.
Correct parameters firstWrong payload numbers make the arm float or sag. Do not use the machine to discover them.

Confirm compatibility, run slow and empty first, keep the physical E-stop in reach — if any one of these is missing, do not start.

7. More traps

All of these happened to me or to friends who also wanted to play with robot arms, roughly by frequency.

1Wire it if you can

A wireless link adds latency and packet loss, so control commands arrive late and the arm itself judders — not the picture, the joints. You also get stutter and “parameters are fine but there is no image”. Run the arm over Ethernet; keep tablets as remote controls only.

2Check the power supply

The same model ships with different supplies (350 W and 288 W in one case). Underpowered units run hot and feel soft. Cheapest, most overlooked item.

3The base is the hard part

A light tripod shakes too. Keeping the tip still usually needs far more counterweight than you expect — a tens-of-kilos steel base removes the source, not just the symptom.

4Match threads and spacing

Use at least three screws on the quick-release plate. Poor machining means it will not fit and you send it back. Measure the flange before ordering.

5Discount the speed spec

The rated speed is ideal; with a camera on an arc you get a fraction of it. Test loaded, not empty and straight.

6Turn camera stabilisation off

In-body and electronic stabilisation fight the arm and produce jelly or counter-motion. Switch them off and let the arm be the stability.

7Leave clearance

I have seen a camera hit a joint (two axes rotated too close) and a wide swing pull the rig over. Block the move on the virtual arm first.

8Choose the quick-release early

Arca, SmallRig and friends are separate ecosystems; the plate must match the one you own. Changing later is painful.

9The same model can hide different hardware

The most invisible pitfall in hardware: one model number, several hardware builds — some use Leaderdrive harmonic reducers with a better motor (common on export units), others ship reduced-spec reducers with domestic motors. Backlash, noise, vibration and service life all differ, while the used price rarely reflects it.
How to tell: ask for photos of the reducer and motor nameplates and get the exact part numbers. If the seller cannot say or will not photograph, price it as the reduced-spec build.

8. Hardware variants: one model number, several machines

Under one model name there are many internal revisions that look identical and shoot very differently. Settle these four before discussing price.

1. Joint travel: double 180 or 360Some CR numeric revisions have ±180° on J4/J5/J6, not ±360°, which limits rolling the camera over. Sellers rarely volunteer this — ask by revision.
2. “A” and “V” are not the sameCR*A and CR*V differ by certification, and the internals differ more than expected: different end buttons, sometimes different brake types. Do not treat the suffix as cosmetic.
3. Brake: electromagnetic or plungerFor film work, prefer electromagnetic. Plunger brakes have visible play, hold position poorly, and in a bad pose can let the camera strike the arm. Fewer end buttons is fine; a wrong brake is not.
4. Reducer: Leaderdrive or reduced specSome units ship Leaderdrive harmonic reducers with a better motor (common on export builds); others use reduced-spec reducers with domestic motors. Backlash, noise and life differ, while the used price rarely reflects it.
How to check

Do not rely on descriptions: ask for photos of the joint nameplates and reducer part numbers, and ask directly whether J4/J5/J6 are 360° or double 180°. If the seller cannot say or will not photograph, price it as the reduced build.

Do not misdiagnose

Collaborative arms use harmonic reducers, so joints have elastic vibration by design — especially the long J2 and J3 axes, where slight vibration is normal. The question is not “does it vibrate” but “how much, and can you live with it”.

9. How to ask for help when something fails

The same fault, asked two ways, gets help ten times faster one way.

How to package it

“The normal channel” really means one zip file containing four things:

① Steps — every action from launching the app to the failure, reproducible;
② Log capture — copy or export diagnostics from the run messages;
③ Project file — the one that fails, ideally openable;
④ Screen recording or photos — landscape, screen and machine in frame.

With all four, most issues are located in the first reply; missing any one of them turns into back-and-forth.

1. Read the message firstThe software and controller usually name the cause. Copy the text; it beats “it does not work”.
2. Export the logRun messages offer “copy” or “export diagnostics”. A log with timestamps and error codes is something the other person can search.
3. Video, screen and machine in frameThe effective request: “film the screen for five seconds, and do not let go at the end of the move.”
4. One controlled experimentUnplug the network and retry; retry unloaded; retry on another computer. Judder over Wi-Fi but not wired means the link; judder wired points at the arm or base.
5. Bring the reproductionModel, firmware, protocol revision, wired or wireless, which step, which project file.
6. Do not jump to conclusionsOpening with “the machine is broken” pushes helpers away. Most cases end in parameters, connections or version mismatch.
7. One variable at a timeSwap supply, cable and software at once and nobody can tell what caused it.
In one lineIf you can describe it as “reproducible steps + a clear symptom”, half the problem is solved.

10. Questions to ask before buying

Copy these straight to the seller.

1. The machineExact model, internal revision, actual J4/J5/J6 travel, brake type — plus reducer and motor part numbers (can they photograph the nameplate?).
2. HistoryRun hours, any drop or rebuild, last service.
3. CompletenessController, cables, base, plate, pendant — all present? Can missing parts be sourced, at what price?
4. Account (turnkey kits only)Can the account be rebound, can the licence transfer, does it need reactivation? Not applicable when buying the arm alone.
5. AcceptanceCan you inspect on site? If not, can a re-inspection window be written into the contract?
6. TransportWho carries the risk, how is it packed, how long to raise issues after delivery?
7. Bring your laptop“May I connect my own computer and software?” For an original arm this is a given. For a turnkey kit it is a litmus test — sellers who agree usually have clean hardware and clean accounts.

11. In one line

The few thousand a used kit saves usually comes back as “the software cannot do it, the parts need remaking, the account cannot be moved” — settle the last three (plate, base, software) before you argue about the arm's price.
