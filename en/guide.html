Buying guide

A film robot arm is not one purchase. It is an arm, a base, a camera plate and a controller, and each of those has its own traps on the used market. Here is the order that works: what it is made of, how to choose, how to inspect, what it costs, what to avoid.

What a film robot arm setup is made of

The first four are essential; the last four decide how pleasant it is to use.

① The armA six-axis jointed arm that sets payload and reach. It ships without a camera mount and without a base — those are separate purchases.
② Base or columnHolds the arm at working height and anchors it to the floor or a cart. On set this is usually an adjustable, counterweighted base; half the stability comes from here.
③ Camera plateBolts to the tool flange and carries the camera or gimbal. Usually custom aluminium with 1/4″, 3/8″ threads or an Arri/Mitchell interface.
④ ControllerThe cabinet that drives the joints. It is matched to the arm — a different model will not work, so never buy the arm without it.
⑤ Power and cablingMotor and encoder cables from arm to cabinet, typically 5–6 m. Longer runs need extensions ordered separately.
⑥ Operator terminalTeach pendant, tablet or a laptop. CineArm runs on the last of those, so a pendant is convenient rather than mandatory.
⑦ Follow focusMotor and controller on the lens gear. In film work focus matters as much as the move, so budget for it separately.
⑧ Transport and counterweightFlight case, lifting points, weights. The arm alone is often 30–40 kg; without a proper case, every location move hurts.

Commonly forgotten: the camera plate is often missing from a used listing and has a lead time when custom made; the base looks like a lump of metal, but get the centre of gravity or stiffness wrong and a long reach will visibly sway.

Choosing a used arm

Do not start with price or cosmetics — start with the job.

01

Work out the total weight

Body, lens, gimbal, quick-release plate and adapter, all of it, then keep 20–30% in reserve. Long lenses move the centre of mass outward, so torque matters more than weight.

02

Then the reach

Reach decides how far the lens travels. A back-and-forth move needs reach that covers both ends, not just the middle.

03

Confirm model and revision

The same model can ship with different joint travel, brake type and protocol. A correct model number does not mean a correct configuration.

04

Inspect in person, not on video

Have the arm extend and retract at the wrist and watch for creep or judder at low speed. Bring your plate and counterweight if you can.

05

Check the whole list

Arm, controller, cables, base, plate, pendant, licence — item by item. Each missing piece turns into lead time and money.

Inspection list

Most of this can be checked in half an hour on site. Whatever you cannot check, write into the contract.

ItemHow to checkWhat should worry you
Joint travel Ask whether J4/J5/J6 are ±180° or ±360°, then drive each axis slowly to both ends in software Travel capped at 180° so the camera cannot roll; a seller who only quotes a model name
Brake type Ask whether it is an electromagnetic or plunger brake; cut power and check whether the tool sags Visible drift at rest; a plunger brake sold as the standard fit
Repeatability Send it to the same position ten times and read back the deviation Deviation you can see; reported and actual positions disagreeing
Low-speed behaviour Slow extend and retract at the wrist, watching the long links Visible judder or creep — note that slight compliance from harmonic drives is normal, not a fault
Noise and play Push each joint gently by hand, listen and feel for slack Clicks or an obviously loose axis, suggesting a drop or a rebuild
Controller state Power up and read alarm history, firmware version and run hours History wiped, hours inconsistent, cabinet and arm serials not matching
Cables and connectors Inspect power and encoder cables along their length, especially at bends Damaged insulation, loose connectors, obvious repairs
Flange interface Measure hole spacing and thread, confirm your plate will fit A bespoke interface with no plate included, meaning new machining
Licensing Confirm the account can be released and the licence transferred Licence tied to the previous owner; cannot be activated after transfer
Protocol Confirm V3 or V4 and whether firmware can be updated Mixed revisions, locked firmware, control software that will not connect after an update

For anything you cannot judge yourself, put “re-inspection within X days of delivery, returnable if it fails” in writing. The expensive part of used gear is not the price — it is discovering the problem after it arrives.

Two items are routinely underestimated: the follow focus system and the base. One decides whether you can pull focus at all, the other whether the frame is steady — together they can approach half the price of the arm.

Common traps

If you hear one of these, ask one more question.

1“Same model” is not the same configuration

One model number can hide 180° or 360° joint travel, different brake types and different protocols. Ask for the configuration in writing.

2“Nine out of ten condition” — without a cabinet

Arm and controller are a pair; a missing cabinet usually means unusable. Ask whether a matching cabinet can be sourced and at what cost.

3Licence still tied to the old account

Some units bind licensing to an account. Confirm it can be released and transferred, or you will own a machine you cannot activate.

4Plunger brake sold as standard

The two brake types behave differently at rest. For film work prefer the electromagnetic version; if you end up with a plunger brake, verify the stop position carefully so the arm cannot contact the camera.

5Video only, no inspection

A video cannot show low-speed judder or joint play. Inspect in person, or at least a live call going through the list — and get a return clause in writing.

6Refurbished dressed as lightly used

Fresh paint and new shells are common. Controller run hours, firmware and alarm history tell you far more than the exterior.

7Plate and base left out

These often fall outside the listing. A custom plate has lead time, and an unsuitable base directly costs you stability — budget for both.

8No plan for transport

These arms start at twenty-odd kilos. Without a flight case and a lifting plan, moving it becomes a recurring cost.

Questions to ask

Ask them directly and keep the answers.

Model and revisionExact model, internal revision, actual J4/J5/J6 travel, brake type, and whether the protocol is V3 or V4.
HistoryRun hours, any drop or rebuild, last service, whether encoder batteries have been replaced.
CompletenessController, cables, base, plate, pendant — all present? If not, can the missing part be sourced and at what price?
LicensingIs the licence valid, can it be released and transferred, does it need reactivation on a new machine?
AcceptanceCan you inspect on site? If not, will they accept a written re-inspection window with a return option?
TransportOriginal packaging or flight case, who carries the risk in transit, how long you have to raise issues after delivery.

This is ordinary practice when buying hardware and has nothing to do with whether you run CineArm — we do not sell arms, only the software that makes them shoot.
