Three steps to a tier

The spec sheets look long, but only three things really decide it.

01

Add up the weight, then leave headroom

Body, lens, gimbal, quick-release plate and cabling — add it all, then keep 20–30% in reserve. Long lenses push the centre of mass outward, so torque matters more than weight.

02

Reach, not height

Reach decides how far the lens can travel. If the move runs back and forth along a line, the reach has to cover both ends — not just the middle.

03

Read the environment

Nova 5 weighs 14 kg and one person can move it; CR10A is 40 kg and CR20A is 73 kg, both needing a heavier, larger base — plan how it gets into the room before you buy.

Buying and inspection notes

These do not show up on a datasheet. Worth confirming before you pay.

1Confirm joint travel first

Some CR units ship with ±180° rather than ±360° on J4/J5/J6, which limits roll and tilt. Confirm the actual configuration with the seller — the model number alone is not enough.

2Brake type changes where it stops

Electromagnetic and plunger brakes behave differently when they come to rest. For film work, prefer the electromagnetic brake version. If you end up with a plunger brake, verify the end position carefully so the arm cannot contact the camera body or lens in motion.

3Test it, don't trust the sheet

Have the arm extend and retract at the wrist and watch for creep or judder at low speed. If you can, bring a mounting plate and counterweight and try the actual unit on site.

4Some vibration is normal

Collaborative joints use harmonic drives, which have inherent compliance. Slight vibration at the long J2 and J3 links in an extended pose is expected. Noticeable vibration is what warrants checking assembly or payload.
